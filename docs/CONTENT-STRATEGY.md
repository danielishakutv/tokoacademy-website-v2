# Toko Academy — Editorial and Keyword Strategy

**Prepared:** 22 September 2026
**For:** Toko Academy Ltd. (tokoacademy.org)
**Scope:** Organic search authority, AI-assistant citation, and visibility to institutional buyers and funders.

---

## How to read this document

This is a working plan, not a report. Sections 1, 4 and 5 are the strategy. Sections 2 and 3 are the
execution list you hand to whoever writes. Sections 6 and 7 are how you keep it alive.

**Three honesty notes before we start, because they shape everything below.**

**One: I cannot see live search volumes.** I do not have access to Google Keyword Planner, Ahrefs or
Semrush. Every difficulty rating in Section 2 is an *inference*, and I say what each one is based on:
who currently occupies the first page of results, how commercially contested the phrase is, whether
big-budget aggregator sites have colonised it, and how narrow the geography is. These are good
inferences — SERP composition is the single most useful signal available and it is directly
observable — but they are not measured volumes. Treat the *ordering* as reliable and the *absolute
numbers* as absent. Within your first month in Google Search Console you will have real impression
data for everything you already rank for, and that beats any third-party estimate.

**Two: every statistic in this document is attributed and dated.** Where I could not verify something
to a primary source, I say so explicitly rather than quietly dropping it in. There is one figure I
deliberately excluded — see "Statistics I could not verify" at the end — and I would rather you
have four solid numbers than twelve shaky ones. This discipline is not fussiness: it is the single
highest-leverage thing you can do for AI citation, for reasons Section 5 explains.

**Three: this strategy assumes you will publish your own data.** The plan does not work if Toko only
recycles what is already online. It works because Toko sits on information nobody else has — cohort
outcomes, real prices, what actually happened in a room full of NSCDC officers, what a Yola parent
pays for holiday coding. That material is the asset. The articles are just the packaging.

## 1. The strategy in a page

### What winning actually looks like

Not "rank number one for digital skills training." That phrase is owned by Lagos-funded competitors
with a decade of backlinks, and chasing it would waste two years.

Winning is this: **when a real decision is being made about digital skills in Nigeria — by a student,
by a training officer, or by a programme manager with a budget — Toko Academy is named.** Concretely,
within twelve months:

- A person in Yola, Jimeta, Mubi, Gombe or Numan who searches for tech training finds Toko first,
  in the map pack and in the organic results, with no meaningful competitor on the page.
- A Nigerian asking ChatGPT, Claude or Perplexity "who trains digital skills in North-East Nigeria"
  or "what does AI training for a Nigerian government agency involve" gets Toko named in the answer,
  because there is a well-structured, well-sourced page for the model to lift from.
- A programme officer at a donor or INGO doing desk research on implementing partners in Adamawa
  finds a credible, evidenced organisational profile — not a brochure — and shortlists it.
- Toko's own published figures start being quoted back at it by other people's articles. That is the
  moment authority becomes self-sustaining.

The mechanism underneath all three is the same, and it is worth stating plainly because it is
counter-intuitive: **you win by being the source, not by being the loudest summariser.** Search
engines and language models both converge on whoever holds the original information. Nigeria has no
shortage of blogs rewriting each other's "top 10 tech skills" listicles. It has a severe shortage of
organisations publishing verifiable first-hand data about what digital skills training actually costs,
who actually completes it, and what actually happens afterwards. Toko can occupy that gap almost
uncontested.

### Who we are writing for

Three audiences, and they want genuinely different things. Most training-provider websites fail
because they write one register of marketing copy and hope it works on everybody.

**The prospective learner.** A 19–32 year-old, often a student, NYSC member, recent graduate or
career-switcher. Frequently on a phone, frequently on limited data. They are price-sensitive and
sceptical, because Nigeria's training market has a real problem with providers who overpromise. They
search in plain, blunt language: *how much is*, *is it worth it*, *requirements for*, *free*, *near
me*. They want to know the fee, the duration, whether it is online or in person, whether the
certificate means anything, and whether anyone actually got a job. Give them a straight answer in the
first paragraph and you have earned the next ten minutes. Bury it under "Empower Your Future" and
they are gone.

**The institutional buyer.** A corporate L&D lead, an SME owner, or — for Toko specifically, and this
is unusual and valuable — a government agency training officer. They are not comparing you on price
alone; they are managing risk. Can you actually deliver? Have you done it before for someone like
them? Will the paperwork survive an audit? They want evidence, references, a clear scope, and
something they can forward to a superior without having to explain it. They search in institutional
vocabulary: *capacity building*, *staff training*, *workforce development*, *consultancy*.

**The funder or partner.** A donor programme officer, an INGO procurement or partnerships lead, a
state government adviser, or a prime contractor looking for a local implementing partner. This is the
audience most training providers ignore entirely, and it is where Toko has a structural advantage
almost nobody else has: a genuine North-East footprint, government-agency delivery already done, a
MEL framework already articulated, safeguarding already published, and a Code.org Global Partner
relationship. This person's questions are: is this organisation real, is it legally constituted, does
it have absorptive capacity, does it understand safeguarding, and can it evidence outcomes? They will
find you through desk research, and increasingly through an AI assistant doing the first pass of that
desk research for them.

A note on the funder audience, honestly stated: **formal procurement does not run through Google.**
UN agencies tender through UNGM and in-tend portals; World Bank-financed work goes through STEP;
prime contractors keep their own partner rosters. I searched for evidence that training-provider
prequalification happens via open search and found essentially none. So content will not win you a
tender directly. What it *does* do is win the due-diligence and shortlisting step that happens before
and around the tender — the moment someone types your name, or types "digital skills partner Adamawa",
and decides in ninety seconds whether you are credible. That moment is absolutely winnable, and it is
worth a great deal.

### The two or three territories Toko should own

Here is where I will be opinionated, because a content strategy that tries to cover everything covers
nothing.

#### Territory 1 — Digital skills in North-East Nigeria *(the moat)*

**Own this completely. It is the most defensible thing you have.**

Nigerian tech content is overwhelmingly Lagos-and-Abuja. When I searched for the North-East tech
ecosystem, the substantive coverage was thin, dated, and mostly about Kano and Kaduna. One piece
([TTLabs via The Condia](https://thecondia.com/northern-nigeria-startup-hotspot-ttlabs/)) put Kano at
25%, Abuja 17.22% and Kaduna 12.92% of Northern startups, with Adamawa at 5.74% and Gombe at 4.31% —
which tells you both that the region is underserved and that essentially nobody is writing about it.

Toko is physically in Jimeta-Yola, delivers in Adamawa, and in January 2026 became a Code.org Global
Partner explicitly to bring computer science and AI education to North-East Nigeria
([Toko Academy press release, 28 January 2026](https://tokoacademy.org/news/press-release-toko-academy-joins-code-org-global-partner-network-to-bring-world-class-ai-and-computer-science-education-to-north-east-nigeria/)).
That is a legitimate, checkable claim to regional authority, and no competitor can copy it by writing
a better blog post.

The territory covers: what is available where, what it costs, who runs it, what the constraints
actually are (power, connectivity, cost, gendered access), and what changes. It serves learners
(*"where can I learn X in Yola"*), buyers (*"who can train our Adamawa office"*) and funders (*"who is
the credible local partner"*) simultaneously. Low competition, high strategic value, permanent.

The one caveat worth naming: the Adamawa State Government launched the **Adamawa Digital Academy**,
which states a target of 100,000 people — 70,000 youths and 30,000 civil servants — across six tracks
in a hybrid model, with NITDA, Microsoft, Google and Huawei named as partners
([digitalacad.adamawastate.gov.ng](https://digitalacad.adamawastate.gov.ng/)). Read that as an
opportunity rather than a threat. A state programme of that size needs delivery partners, and the
organisation that has already published the definitive guide to digital skills in Adamawa is the
obvious one to call. Write about it generously and accurately.

#### Territory 2 — Practical AI for Nigerian workplaces and institutions *(the wedge)*

**This is where the money and the momentum are, and Toko already has proof.**

The demand signal here is unusually strong and comes from a good source. Google's *Our Life with AI*
study, run with Ipsos across roughly 1,000 Nigerian adults in the online population between 22
September and 10 October 2025, found **88% of Nigerian adults had used an AI chatbot** against a
global average of 62%; **93%** use AI to learn or understand complex topics (global average 74%); and
**80%** use it to explore business ideas or career changes, against a global average of 42%
([reported by Pulse Nigeria, 21 January 2026](https://www.pulse.ng/story/google-report-shows-nigerians-use-ai-for-learning-and-business-more-than-most-of-the-world-2026012108564668941)).

Nigerians are already using AI heavily and mostly teaching themselves. That is a training market
hiding in plain sight.

On the institutional side, the Federal Government is actively pushing AI capability into the public
service — UNESCO ran AI literacy training for senior civil servants from 6–8 May 2026
([UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/advancing-nigerias-digital-transformation-unesco-trains-civil-service-ai-and-digital-government)),
and the Minister of Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy has publicly urged public servants
to build AI and digital skills
([News Agency of Nigeria](https://nannews.ng/tijani-urges-public-servants-to-build-ai-digital-skills-for-better-service-delivery/)).

And Toko has already done the work. Structured AI and digital literacy training for officers across
NSCDC, the Nigeria Police Force, FRSC and NMDPRA in November 2025; a free AI skills workshop for
NASSCO staff at the State Operations Coordinating Unit in Yola on 12 February 2026; *From AI Users to
AI Builders* in February 2026; and the *Zero to Live* weekend workshop. **Almost nobody in Nigeria can
write a credible first-hand account of teaching AI to a room of uniformed officers. Toko can.** That
is a content asset worth more than fifty generic "What is ChatGPT" posts.

#### Territory 3 — How Nigeria's digital skills system actually works *(the reference work)*

**This is the authority play, and the one most likely to get Toko cited by AI assistants.**

Nigeria has an extraordinary tangle of overlapping publicly-funded skills programmes: 3MTT, DL4ALL,
the Digital Training Academy, NDE's employment scheme, the World Bank-supported IDEAS TVET project,
ITF/SIWES, SMEDAN, the Adamawa Digital Academy, and donor programmes like Mastercard Foundation ×
Tech4Dev's TTS Nigeria. Every one of these is searched for constantly by young Nigerians. Almost all
the content about them is low-quality scraper blogs with wrong dates and dead application links.

There is no clear, current, honestly-sourced map of this system anywhere. Toko should build it and
maintain it.

This does triple duty. Students searching "free tech training Nigeria 2026" land on an accurate,
useful page. Funders and government staff doing landscape research find an organisation that
demonstrably understands the ecosystem — which is exactly the signal that gets you shortlisted as a
partner. And AI assistants, which love well-structured reference material with named sources and
dates, get something worth quoting.

It is also the territory with the strongest "moat through maintenance": a page that is genuinely
updated every quarter beats a hundred abandoned ones, and competitors rarely sustain that.

#### What Toko should *not* chase

Generic tutorial content — "Python tutorial for beginners", "what is HTML", "SQL basics". W3Schools,
freeCodeCamp, GeeksforGeeks and now AI assistants themselves have permanently won this. You cannot
out-rank them, the traffic does not convert to enrolment, and every hour spent there is an hour not
spent on something defensible. Write tutorials only when they are *specifically* Nigerian or
*specifically* tied to your own teaching — see brief 22 for the shape that works.

## 3. Thirty article briefs

Ordered by priority. The first six are marked **START HERE** — they are the ones that pay back fastest
and unlock the internal-linking structure everything else depends on. Word counts are targets, not
rules; the number that matters is whether the question is fully answered.

A standing instruction for every brief: **the first 60 words must answer the title question directly.**
No throat-clearing, no "In today's fast-paced digital world." This is the single biggest quality lever
you have, it is what makes a passage quotable by an AI assistant, and it is what stops a reader on
expensive mobile data from leaving.

### 1. START HERE — Digital Skills Training in Yola and Adamawa State: The Complete 2026 Guide

- **Intent:** Local, high commercial intent. Someone in or near Adamawa deciding where to train.
- **Primary keyword:** digital skills training in Yola
- **Secondary:** tech training Adamawa State · computer training centre Jimeta · coding classes Yola · tech bootcamp Northern Nigeria
- **Outline:**
  - Direct answer: what training is available in Adamawa right now, in one paragraph with a table.
  - Every option, fairly presented: Toko Academy, the Adamawa Digital Academy, federal programmes open to Adamawa residents (3MTT, DL4ALL, NDE), and university/polytechnic routes.
  - What it costs — a real price table, including free options.
  - The constraints nobody writes about: power, data cost, transport within Jimeta-Yola, and how each provider handles them.
  - How to choose: a short decision guide by situation (secondary leaver, undergraduate, NYSC, working adult, civil servant).
  - Getting there — address, campus, timetable, how to enrol.
- **Length:** 2,000–2,500 words.
- **Why it is genuinely useful:** No such page exists. Every element is verifiable and locally true. It is the article a Yola parent or student would actually forward to someone.
- **Internal links:** Cornerstone A (it *is* Cornerstone A's lead article, or merge them), /courses, /schedules, /kids, Article 2, Article 4.
- **Proprietary asset:** Photographs of the actual Jimeta campus and a real cohort. The live timetable. Toko's real published prices. An embedded map. This is the page that most needs to look unmistakably real.

## 5. Being cited by AI assistants

This section is longer and more cautious than the ones you will read elsewhere, because most published advice on this topic is vendor marketing built on correlational studies. I have separated what is well-evidenced from what is plausible from what is sold as fact and is not. Where I am uncertain, I say so.

### First, the uncomfortable part: what the platforms themselves say

Google published an official guide to optimising for its generative AI features (developers.google.com, May 2026). It states, in its own words, that *"the best practices for SEO continue to be relevant because our generative AI features on Google Search are rooted in our core Search ranking and quality systems,"* that *"you don't need to create new machine readable files, AI text files, markup, or Markdown to appear in Google Search... Google Search ignores them,"* that *"there's no requirement to break your content into tiny pieces for AI to better understand it,"* and that *"seeking inauthentic 'mentions' across the web isn't as helpful as it might seem."* Its companion page on AI features adds that *"there are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode."*

**Read that plainly: for Google's AI surfaces, the route in is ordinary good SEO.** AI Overviews and AI Mode retrieve from the Search index. If Toko is not indexed and ranking, no amount of AI-specific formatting will help.

The GEO-specific layer matters more for the non-Google surfaces — ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude — which use different retrieval stacks and, as the data below shows, cite startlingly different sources.

### What the research actually shows

**The best-known study.** Aggarwal et al., *"GEO: Generative Engine Optimization,"* presented at KDD '24 (arXiv:2311.09735), tested nine content modifications against a 10,000-query benchmark. The findings that matter:

| Modification | Effect on visibility |
|---|---|
| Adding direct quotations | strongest positive |
| Adding verifiable statistics | strong positive |
| Citing sources | strong positive |
| Improving fluency | moderate positive |
| **Keyword stuffing** | **negative** |

The paper's headline claim is that these techniques can boost visibility *"by up to 40%"* in generative engine responses, with effects varying by domain. Notably, adding citations helped lower-ranked sources far more than top-ranked ones — the authors frame this as democratising, which is encouraging for a small organisation.

**The essential caveat, which almost nobody quotes.** A 2026 critical survey of 45 GEO studies (arXiv:2607.14035) found that these gains are *conditional on the source already being retrieved into the model's context.* They *"establish neither organic discoverability nor durable traffic effects."* Subsequent benchmarks were harsher still: one found only 3 of 54 method-domain combinations significantly positive, another found most generic heuristics neutral or negative, and one found that optimising a page body alone *reduced* its presence in the top results after reranking. The survey's blunt conclusion: *"Claims about GEO return on investment clearly outstrip the academic evidence."*

**So the honest framing is this: these techniques are prominence levers, not discovery levers.** They affect how much of your page gets quoted once it has been retrieved. They do not get you retrieved. Getting retrieved is relevance plus authority plus ordinary search visibility.

**What does correlate with being cited.** Ahrefs analysed 75,000 brands against AI Overview visibility (May 2025). The strongest correlate by a wide margin was *branded web mentions* (Spearman 0.664) — roughly three times stronger than backlinks (0.218). A later extension found YouTube mentions correlating even higher. Ahrefs themselves caution that correlation is not causation and that all the factors studied were moderate-to-weak. But the direction is consistent and it points somewhere useful: **being talked about by other people matters more than technical optimisation.** Note the tension with Google's warning about "inauthentic mentions" — the correlate is *earned editorial mention*, not bought placement.

**Platforms diverge sharply.** Analysis by Profound of a very large citation sample found ChatGPT's single most-cited source is Wikipedia, Perplexity's is Reddit, and Google AI Overviews lean on Reddit, YouTube and Quora. When the same 100,000 prompts were run through ChatGPT and Perplexity, **only about 11% of cited domains appeared in both.** "Get cited by AI" is therefore not one objective. For Toko I would prioritise **Google's surfaces and ChatGPT**, and treat Perplexity as a secondary benefit rather than a target, because winning Perplexity means building a community footprint on Reddit that does not fit Toko's audience or budget.

**Recency matters.** Multiple independent analyses find AI-cited content is substantially newer than typical organic results. This is one of the cheapest advantages available: date your pages, update them visibly, and maintain the reference pages on a real schedule.

### What to actually do

**1. Get the fundamentals right first, because they are the entry ticket.**
Indexable, fast, mobile-sound, properly titled, internally linked. Everything below is worthless without this. Google has said outright that its AI features run off the core index.

**2. Fix crawler access — and check it carefully, because there is a specific trap.**

The distinction almost everyone gets wrong: **OpenAI's `GPTBot` is for model training; `OAI-SearchBot` is what governs whether you can be cited in ChatGPT search.** Blocking GPTBot does not remove you from ChatGPT citations, and allowing it does not get you cited. The same split exists at Anthropic (`ClaudeBot` for training, `Claude-SearchBot` for search) and Perplexity (`PerplexityBot` surfaces and links sites; Perplexity states it is not used for foundation-model training).

At Google, **`Google-Extended` controls Gemini and Vertex AI grounding and does *not* affect AI Overviews or AI Mode.** Many sites blocked it in 2023–24 believing otherwise and lost Gemini visibility for no benefit. Googlebot is the control for AI Overviews.

**Toko's current position here is genuinely good and should be protected.** As of this writing the duplicate robots configuration has been resolved — the static `public/robots.txt` has been removed and `src/app/robots.ts` is the single source of truth. That file now allows AI crawlers *explicitly and by name* (including `OAI-SearchBot`, `Claude-SearchBot` and `PerplexityBot`, which are the three that actually govern citation eligibility), and it no longer blocks `/_next/`, which the old static file did. Blocking `/_next/` is a common and damaging mistake: it stops Googlebot fetching the site's own JavaScript and CSS, so the page is rendered and scored without them.

Two things to keep an eye on. First, that named-crawler list is the kind of thing a future "let's block the AI scrapers" edit removes by accident — it is worth defending deliberately. Second, allowing `Google-Extended` is correct, but be clear internally about what it does: it governs Gemini and Vertex AI grounding and has **no** effect on AI Overviews or AI Mode, which are controlled by Googlebot. Several sites blocked it believing otherwise and lost Gemini visibility for nothing.

Also verify that no CDN or WAF is blocking AI crawlers at the edge — several providers now ship AI-bot blocking on by default, and a permissive robots.txt is irrelevant if the request never arrives. And check that no page carries a `max-snippet` restriction, which can silently suppress AI-feature appearance.

**3. Write so that any single section can be lifted out and still make sense.**

This is the one structural recommendation the evidence genuinely supports, and the reasoning matters. Retrieval systems work on passages, not whole pages. The chunk that gets retrieved is the chunk that gets quoted. A section beginning *"As we saw above, this means..."* cannot be quoted, because out of context it means nothing.

So: each H2 introduces a self-contained unit. The first sentence under it answers the heading. Names, dates and figures are repeated rather than pronouned — "Toko Academy's 2026 cohort" not "our cohort that year."

Note Google's counterpoint, though: *"there's no requirement to break your content into tiny pieces."* Do not shred the page. Write coherent sections that happen to stand alone — which is simply good writing.

**4. Use question-shaped headings that match how people actually ask.**

*"How much does a data analysis course cost in Nigeria?"* is better than *"Pricing."* The evidence on this is moderate rather than strong, but the mechanism is sound: the strongest reproducible factor in citation is explicit alignment between the question and the text. If someone asks an assistant that question, a page containing that exact question, answered directly underneath with a number, is the easiest thing in the world to quote.

**5. Publish named statistics with a source and a date — including your own.**

This is where the evidence converges most clearly. Statistics, citations and quotations are the units that get extracted. But there is a strategic point beyond the mechanics: **most statistics on the Nigerian digital-skills market are recycled from a handful of originals.** Toko can be an original. Publishing real cohort data with a stated denominator and methodology (Articles 15 and 29) makes Toko the primary source rather than another site quoting one — and primary sources are what both journalists and language models prefer.

Format every figure the same way: **the number, what it measures, who produced it, and when.** "Nigeria's cybersecurity workforce gap is 90.6% (NITDA Director-General Kashifu Abdullahi, National Cybersecurity Conference, July 2025)" is quotable. "Nigeria has a huge cybersecurity skills gap" is not.

**6. Use tables for anything comparative.**

Prices, programme comparisons, state-by-state provision, course options. Tables are dense, unambiguous and easy to extract. They are also genuinely better for a reader on a phone than three paragraphs of prose describing the same thing.

**7. Schema markup — do it, but for the right reason.**

I want to be careful here, because schema is the most over-sold item in this field. Ahrefs ran a controlled difference-in-differences study on 1,885 pages that added JSON-LD against 4,000 matched controls (May 2026) and found **no meaningful lift in AI citations** — a small negative effect on AI Overviews, and changes indistinguishable from noise on AI Mode and ChatGPT. The widely-circulated claim that cited pages are "three times more likely to have schema" is correlational: schema marks out professionally-maintained sites, which get cited for other reasons.

Google's own position: *"there's also no special schema.org structured data that you need to add"* for AI features, though it remains *"a good idea... as part of your overall SEO strategy."* Microsoft's Bing has stated more positively that schema helps its models understand content, but that single remark has been contested and should not carry a strategy on its own.

**So: implement schema as entity infrastructure and correct semantic description — not as a citation growth tactic.** Specifically:

- `EducationalOrganization` on the homepage and About page, with `sameAs` pointing to every verified Toko profile — LinkedIn, YouTube, Facebook, Google Business Profile. This is entity disambiguation: it helps systems understand that these accounts are all one organisation. That is a different and more durable mechanism than citation.
- `LocalBusiness` with consistent name, address and phone, matching a claimed and complete Google Business Profile exactly. For a physical campus in Jimeta-Yola this is genuinely valuable for local and map results.
- `Course` and `CourseInstance` for every course. Note that Google retired the "course info" rich result in 2025 while course list/carousel markup remains supported — verify current status before relying on any rich result, as this area changes frequently. Mark it up anyway; it is the correct description of the thing.
- `Article` with real `Person` author markup and genuine credentials.
- `FAQPage` — keep it where you have it, but **do not invest in adding it for Google rich results, which have been removed.** It remains valid Schema.org and is harmless.

**A concrete gap to fix:** the site currently has JSON-LD on courses, events, gallery, news, partners and zero2live — but **not on the homepage, About, Corporate, Impact, Thematic Areas or Kids.** Those are exactly the pages that define the organisation as an entity. Fix that first.

**8. `llms.txt` — include it if you like, but know what it is.**

Proposed by Jeremy Howard in September 2024 as a curated Markdown index for AI consumption. The honest position: **there is no credible public evidence that any major AI search system reads it, and Google has explicitly said it ignores such files.** OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity's own crawler documentation makes no mention of it.

It costs an hour and carries no risk, and it is a tidy artefact for agentic tools. Add it if you want. **Do not let anyone sell it to you as a visibility tactic, and do not put it in a plan as one.**

**9. Earn genuine third-party mentions — this is the highest-leverage item.**

Given that branded mentions correlate more strongly than any technical factor, the most valuable AI-visibility work is not on the website at all. It is:
- Being quoted in Nigerian tech and national media. Techpoint, BusinessDay, Daily Trust, Leadership and Vanguard all cover this beat and need credible regional sources. An organisation publishing real North-East data is exactly what a journalist on deadline wants.
- **YouTube.** It correlates unusually strongly with AI citation across surfaces, and Toko already produces teaching material and runs events. Publishing workshop recordings, student showcases and short explainers is close to free.
- Being listed accurately in directories, partner pages and programme registers.

**Crucially: earned, not manufactured.** Buying guest posts on Nigerian news domains is exactly the pattern Google's site-reputation-abuse policy targets, and it is what Google means by "inauthentic mentions." Do not do it.

**10. Be consistent about who you are.**

Use the same organisation name, the same address format, the same founding details everywhere — site, Google Business Profile, LinkedIn, partner listings, press releases. Entity resolution is how a model knows that "Toko Academy," "Toko Academy Ltd" and "Toko Academy, Yola" are one thing. Inconsistency fragments the evidence.

### What to avoid

**Unsourced statistics.** The fastest way to be useless to an AI assistant — and to a journalist — is to state a number nobody can trace. If you cannot name the source and date, cut it.

**Scaled thin content.** Google's scaled-content-abuse policy is explicit that the *method* is irrelevant: AI-written content is permitted, but producing many pages without adding value violates the policy whether a human or a machine wrote them. Thirty genuinely useful articles beat three hundred spun ones, and the three hundred carry real risk.

**Keyword stuffing.** The GEO research found it actively counterproductive in generative engines — the only tested modification that made things worse.

**Bought mentions and parasite placements.** See above. Real risk, and it undermines the authentic authority that is Toko's actual advantage.

**Data locked in PDFs.** Toko's profile PDF is good, but anything you want quoted must also exist as HTML on a page. Put the figures in the page and offer the PDF as a download.

**Content that needs JavaScript to appear.** If the substance only renders client-side, some crawlers will not see it. Toko's Next.js setup mostly handles this well — keep it that way for article content.

**Hidden instructions aimed at models.** Invisible text or prompt injections intended to manipulate AI outputs are being sold as a service. They are manipulation, they carry reputational and policy risk, and they are beneath an organisation whose entire positioning is credibility.

**Over-claiming in measurement.** See Section 7 — AI answers are highly non-deterministic, and a single lucky result proves nothing.

## 6. Publishing cadence and process

### Be realistic about volume

A small team that commits to three articles a week will produce four good ones and then stop. The plan below assumes limited hours and prioritises consistency over volume, because consistency is what compounds.

**Target: four substantial articles a month, plus maintenance.** Thirty briefs at that rate is roughly seven to eight months of work. That is the right pace.

A workable monthly rhythm:
- **Two "anchor" articles** — 1,500+ words, from the brief list, in priority order.
- **One "asset" piece** — shorter, built around something Toko already has: a workshop, a cohort result, a student project, an event. These are fast because the material exists.
- **One "maintenance" slot** — updating a reference page (Cornerstone B, Articles 2, 11, 23) rather than writing new. This is the slot everyone skips and the one that protects the most value.

### Who does what

Three roles, not necessarily three people.

**The owner of the material — the founder or a senior trainer.** Nobody else can supply what makes this strategy work: the real numbers, what actually happened in the NSCDC room, why a cohort underperformed. This person should not be writing drafts. They should be spending **45 minutes per article** in a recorded conversation answering the brief's questions, and **15 minutes** reviewing the draft for accuracy. That is an hour an article — four hours a month. That is sustainable; drafting is not.

**The writer.** Turns the recording and the brief into a draft, does the desk research, sources every statistic, builds the tables. This is the bulk of the work and can be an internal junior staffer or a freelancer. The brief in Section 3 is deliberately detailed enough to hand over directly.

**The publisher.** Formatting, internal links, images, schema, metadata, scheduling, and the distribution steps below. Half a day a week.

### The quality gate

Before anything publishes, five checks. Make this a literal checklist — quality collapses when it lives in someone's head.

1. **Does the opening answer the title question in the first 60 words?** If not, rewrite the opening.
2. **Is every statistic attributed with a source and a date?** If a figure cannot be traced, remove it. No exceptions — this is the discipline the whole strategy rests on.
3. **Does it contain something only Toko could publish?** A photograph, a number, a template, a first-hand account. If it does not, it is filler and should not go out.
4. **Are there at least three internal links, and does something link back to it?** An orphaned article is invisible.
5. **Would a sceptical reader find it honest?** Does it acknowledge what Toko cannot do, and treat competitors and free programmes fairly?

### Distribution, which matters as much as publishing

Publishing is not distribution, and given that third-party mentions correlate more strongly with AI citation than anything technical, this half is arguably the more valuable one.

For each article: LinkedIn post from the founder's personal account (reach beats the company page); WhatsApp to relevant groups and to past cohorts; the Toko newsletter; and, for the student-facing pieces, the relevant **Nairaland** board — Nairaland ranks strongly on exactly the "is it worth it" and "where can I learn" queries Toko wants, and it is a distribution channel as much as a competitor. Participate honestly; do not spam.

For the substantial ones — particularly Articles 15, 16 and 29 — send a short, specific note to named journalists at Techpoint, BusinessDay, Daily Trust and Leadership. They cover this beat and need regional sources with real data.

And record video. Even a five-minute phone recording of a trainer explaining an article's core point, posted to YouTube and linked from the article, is worth doing given how strongly YouTube presence correlates with AI citation.

### Maintenance

- **Quarterly:** review every reference page (Cornerstone B, Articles 2, 11, 23) and update the "last verified" date. This is non-negotiable; a stale reference page is worse than none.
- **Twice yearly:** refresh prices and salary figures, which move fast in naira.
- **Annually:** the outcomes report (Article 29).
- **Ongoing:** when a workshop happens, photograph it properly and note two or three concrete details the same day. The material for half these articles is generated in the ordinary course of business and then lost.

## 7. Measuring it

### Watch these

**Search Console impressions by topic cluster, monthly.** Not rankings — impressions. They move first and they tell you whether Google is starting to consider you relevant for a territory. Group the queries into the six keyword groups in Section 2 and track each as a block.

**Position for the twenty "realistic 6-month win" terms.** Check monthly, not daily. These are your evidence that the local and institutional strategy is working. If the Yola and Adamawa terms are not on page one by month four, something is technically wrong — investigate rather than write more.

**Branded search volume.** How many people search "Toko Academy" and its variants. This is the closest available proxy for the branded-mention signal that correlates most strongly with AI citation, and it is a genuine measure of authority rather than traffic.

**Enquiries, attributed.** Add one question to every enquiry form and every discovery call: *how did you hear about us?* Include "an AI assistant recommended you" as an option. This is currently the most reliable way to detect AI referral, because much AI citation is invisible in analytics.

**Referral traffic from AI domains.** Look for `chatgpt.com`, `perplexity.ai`, `claude.ai`, `copilot.microsoft.com` in your referrers. Volumes will be small; the trend is what matters.

**A monthly AI citation check — done properly.** This is where most people fool themselves. AI answers are highly non-deterministic: research measuring this found cited-source overlap between repeated runs of the same query as low as roughly a third, and repeated runs changing their answer in a meaningful share of cases even at temperature zero. **A single query proves nothing, in either direction.**

So: keep a fixed panel of about fifteen questions — "where can I learn digital skills in North-East Nigeria," "who trains Nigerian government staff on AI," "how much does tech training cost in Nigeria," and so on. Run each **at least five times**, across ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity and Google AI Mode, on the same day each month. Record how often Toko appears. Track the *rate*, not individual results. Thirty minutes a month, and it is the only honest way to measure this.

**Google Business Profile actions.** Calls, direction requests, website clicks. For a physical campus these are high-intent signals and they respond faster than organic rankings.

**Set up the free first-party reporting.** Google Search Console has a generative-AI performance view, and Bing Webmaster Tools added an AI Performance report in 2026 covering Copilot citations. Both are free, both are first-party, and both beat any third-party estimate. Do this in week one.

### Ignore these

**Domain Authority, Domain Rating and similar scores.** Third-party inventions. They do not appear in any ranking system and optimising for them optimises for a vendor's model of reality.

**Raw pageviews as a headline.** Two hundred readers from Adamawa searching for local training are worth more than twenty thousand from a viral listicle. Judge traffic by segment and intent.

**Bounce rate.** Someone who reads your price table, gets the number they needed and leaves has been served perfectly. Bounce rate would score that as failure.

**Daily rank tracking.** Results fluctuate constantly and daily checking produces anxiety and bad decisions. Monthly.

**Total backlink count.** The research points to earned editorial mentions, not raw link volume. Chasing the number invites exactly the tactics that carry risk.

**Any single AI answer, good or bad.** See above. One query is noise.

**Vendor "AI visibility scores."** No third party has access to these systems' internals — Google has said so explicitly. Treat such scores as marketing.

### What good looks like at six and twelve months

**At six months:** page-one positions for most Yola and Adamawa terms; the first six articles indexed and accumulating impressions; Google Business Profile claimed and producing calls; a handful of enquiries traceable to content; Toko appearing occasionally in AI answers about North-East Nigeria specifically.

**At twelve months:** the North-East territory substantially owned; Cornerstone B established as the reference page for Nigerian skills programmes; the outcomes report published and cited by at least one journalist; institutional enquiries arriving through content rather than only through networks; consistent appearance in AI answers for regional and government-training questions.

**What will not have happened by twelve months, and should not be promised:** ranking nationally for "coding bootcamp in Nigeria." That territory is held by a foreign programmatic publisher and a cluster of content farms with enormous page counts. It is not where Toko's advantage lies, and pursuing it would cost the things that are winnable.
